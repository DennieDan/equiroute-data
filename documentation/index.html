How JalanLens measures accessible streets

JalanLens turns street imagery, map context, public feedback, and accessibility standards into evidence that city teams can act on.

The problem

Accessibility barriers are often visible, but not organized.

A missing ramp, narrow footpath, rough surface, blocked tactile paving, or unsafe crossing can decide whether a route is usable. JalanLens makes those observations searchable, comparable, and grounded in real street evidence.

Before the lens

People experience the city one obstacle at a time.

Reports are scattered, inspections are slow, and accessibility decisions often lack visible, street-level context.

The JalanLens moment

A shared picture of who can move safely.

The platform connects accessibility scores, street-view evidence, persona routes, and public feedback into one civic operating view.

After the lens

Authorities can respond with evidence, not guesses.

Each segment becomes a clear case: what is wrong, who is affected, what evidence supports it, and what response should happen next.

    Prototype caveat

    The demo uses geospatial proxy measurements where direct field measurements are not yet available.

    Data sources

    • accessibility_world.geojson — street parts, POIs, accessibility metrics, personas, and standards metadata.
    • buildings_clementi.geojson — nearby building footprints for Earth context.
    • threejs_3d_roads.json — lightweight 3D road and corridor overlay data.
    • data/photo_feature_instances_cv.json — localized street-view accessibility features from the CV workflow.

    What the map shows

    • 25 m-scale footpath street parts with individual accessibility scorecards.
    • Earth satellite view for area context, plus street-view inspection for ground evidence.
    • Accessibility features such as ramps, shelters, tactile paving, bus stops, and obstacles.
    • Persona route simulation for public-agency review of different mobility needs.

    How to read the scores

    1. Select a footpath segment from the top bar or map labels.
    2. Use the scorecard to compare width, surface quality, slopes, vertical changes, and nearby hazards.
    3. Switch to Street View when a segment needs visible ground evidence.
    4. Use persona agents to test whether a route is practical for wheelchair users, cane users, older adults, or parents with strollers.
